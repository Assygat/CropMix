Decision Lab

Plan for a short water year.

Set how much irrigation water the region receives, choose the rules a plan must respect, and the optimiser finds the crop mix that keeps the most land in production. It then explains which rules decided the answer and what one more unit of water would be worth. Everything here is a planning scenario built on the same FAO-56 model as the rest of the site. It is not a forecast and not an allocation decision.

  • Linear programming, exact optimum
  • Solved in your browser, checked against SciPy/HiGHS
  • Water shares and rules are user scenarios
  • Not validated

01

Water-scarcity planner

Start from the official 2025 crop areas (188 094 ha). The model needs 7.30 km³ of river withdrawal for them in an average year (2010–2025 climate, base efficiencies). Choose how much of that is available. The optimiser then picks the area of each crop group. Water use, land, the area kept for each crop and the food-crop minimum are hard limits: they are never broken silently. When no plan can meet them, the page says so and shows which limit to relax and by how much.

Areas: official 2025 statistics. Water per hectare: model (FAO-56 ETc, USDA SCS effective rainfall, base field and canal efficiencies). Water share, rules and climate changes: user scenarios. Production is shown only where a regional yield exists in the data.

02

Climate stress tests

Six scenarios recompute crop water demand with the same Penman–Monteith, crop-coefficient and effective-rainfall functions. Nothing is rescaled after the fact. The drought year is an observed year: the 2010–2025 year with the highest modelled demand. The heat and rainfall changes are sensitivity experiments chosen by us, not climate projections. Warming is applied to daily maximum and minimum temperature at constant relative humidity. Available water is a share of the normal-year demand of the 2025 crop mix.

From outputs/stress_scenarios.csv and outputs/stress_emergency.csv. “Area kept” is the optimiser’s answer with the protective rules (each crop keeps at least 50 % of its 2025 area and grows by at most 30 %; food crops keep at least 80 % of their area).

03

Reference results

The same scarcity levels, solved in Python with SciPy’s HiGHS solver for the two rule sets. The planner above reproduces these numbers. As a reference rule, we compare each plan with a uniform cut, where every crop loses the same share of its area as the water. That rule is a benchmark, not a description of current practice.

04

Why same-land savings are small

The site’s original optimiser keeps the total sown area fixed. With the moderate rules it saves 3.4 % of the water. To find out why the number is that small, we relaxed one rule at a time and solved again. The table below shows that the limit comes from how the problem is set up, not from the solver.

05

Economic coefficients

The gross-value objective needs a yield, a price and (optionally) a cost for every crop group. The data contain some of these: regional yields for five groups, two regional prices and four national average prices. They contain no production costs and no yields for wheat, fodder or other grains. Missing values are left empty. They are never filled in for you. Enter your own figures to use the gross-value objective. Results computed from values you entered are assumption-based estimates, not economic forecasts.

06

How the optimiser works

The decision variables are the areas xc (ha) of the eight crop groups. Every relation is linear, so the problem is a linear programme. Its optimum is exact and can be checked; no machine learning is involved.

Water-scarcity allocationlinear programme

maximise ∑cxc  (or another objective, below) subject to ∑cwcxc≤s·W0 ∑cxc≤∑cxc0,∑c∈Fxc≥f∑c∈Fxc0 rxc0≤xc≤(1+d)xc0
  • wc: river withdrawal per hectare from the model (m³/ha). W0: demand of the 2025 mix in an average year (7.30 km³). s: available share, a user scenario. x0: 2025 areas. F: food crops (rice, wheat, potato, vegetables, melons). f, r, d: rule parameters (assumed).
  • Objectives: keep the most cropped area; keep the most food-crop area; use the least water; or, when all economic coefficients are entered, maximise gross value Σ (Yc·Pc − Cc)·xc. When several plans tie on the objective, the one that uses less water is chosen.
  • Binding constraint: a rule that the plan meets exactly. Relaxing it would improve the objective. Shadow price: how much the objective improves per unit of relaxation, for example hectares kept per additional million m³ of water. It holds only until another rule starts to bind, so it is a local value, not a price you could charge.
  • Pareto curve: for each water share, the best achievable area. No plan can keep more area with the same water; that is why one plan cannot be best on water and area at once.

What this does not model. Water is applied at full crop demand or not at all: there is no deficit irrigation and no yield response. Fallow land uses no water. The region is one unit, with no canals, districts or timing within the season. The per-hectare coefficients carry the same uncertainty as the rest of the model (see the Monte Carlo range on the Data & Evidence page). Plans are corner solutions of a linear model and are meant to start a discussion, not to replace one.
