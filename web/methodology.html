Methodology

From daily weather to river water.

CropMix Twin follows the FAO-56 crop water method day by day, adds the losses between the river and the plant, and sums the result over the region’s crop areas. This page sets out each step, the equations behind it and the assumptions it rests on.

  • Daily time step, 2010–2025
  • 8 crop groups
  • 36 efficiency scenarios
  • Linear programming optimiser

01

Model architecture

The model answers one planning question: for a given crop mix, how much water has to be taken from the Syr Darya to meet the crops’ demand, and how would a different mix change that amount?

  1. Daily climateTemperature, humidity, wind and radiation, 2010–2025.NASA POWER
  2. Reference ET₀Evaporative demand of a standard grass surface.FAO-56 Eq. 6
  3. Crop demand ETcET₀ scaled by a crop coefficient for each day of the season.ETc = ET₀ × Kc
  4. Field and riverEffective rainfall, field losses, canal losses; flooding for rice.36 scenarios
  5. Region and optimiserCoefficients × crop areas; least-water crop mix.Linear programming

Steps 1–4 run in Python (src/) for every crop, year and efficiency scenario. They produce seasonal coefficients in cubic metres per hectare. The website and the simulator do not repeat these calculations: they only multiply the published coefficients by crop areas. The browser code and the Python results agree to within 10−12 km³, which an automated test checks for every scenario.

Each modelled volume is written W(A, C, E): the crop areas of year A, the climate of year C (or Cmean, defined below) and an efficiency scenario E. Naming all three keeps comparisons honest. A 2024 result, for example, uses the areas and the climate of 2024.

Variables and units

Water is handled as a depth (millimetres) while it is computed per crop and as a volume (cubic metres) once it is attached to an area. One millimetre of water over one hectare is ten cubic metres.

03

Reference evapotranspiration

ET₀ is the evaporative demand of a well-watered reference grass. CropMix Twin computes it every day with the FAO-56 Penman–Monteith equation (Allen et al., 1998).

FAO-56 Penman–MonteithEq. 6, daily time step

ET0= 0.408⁢Δ⁢(Rn−G) +γ⁢900T+273⁢u2⁢(es−ea) Δ+γ⁢(1+0.34⁢u2)
Rn
net radiation at the surface, MJ m⁻² day⁻¹ (from incoming solar radiation, albedo 0.23 and net long-wave radiation, Eq. 38–39)
G
soil heat flux; taken as 0 for a daily step (Eq. 42)
T
mean daily air temperature at 2 m, °C
u2
wind speed at 2 m, m s⁻¹
es−ea
vapour pressure deficit, kPa (ea from mean relative humidity, Eq. 19)
Δ,γ
slope of the vapour pressure curve and psychrometric constant, kPa °C⁻¹ (Eq. 13 and 8)

The weather comes from NASA POWER for the grid point 44.8° N, 65.5° E (elevation 122 m), next to Kyzylorda. NASA POWER is a gridded product built from the MERRA-2 reanalysis and the CERES satellite product, covering a 0.5° × 0.625° cell. It is not a station record. For a cross-check, temperatures were compared with the Kyzylorda weather station (GSOD 38062). In April–October, NASA daily maximum temperature runs 1.2 °C lower and minimum temperature 1.7 °C higher than the station, and NASA wind is about 16 % stronger than the station estimate. The model is not corrected for these differences. The wind effect is tested in the sensitivity analysis instead (see Data & Evidence).

Two simplifications follow FAO-56 guidance. Humidity is available only as a daily mean, so actual vapour pressure uses Eq. 19, which FAO-56 rates as less accurate than Eq. 17. Negative winter values of ET₀ are set to zero.

04

Crop coefficients and four growth stages

A crop uses more or less water than reference grass depending on its stage of growth. FAO-56 describes the season with four stages: initial, development, mid-season and late season. The crop coefficient is constant in the initial and mid-season stages and changes linearly in between (FAO-56 Eq. 66).

Crop coefficient on day iFAO-56 Eq. 66

Kc,i= { Kiniinitial stage Kini+i−LiniLdev⁢(Kmid−Kini)development Kmidmid-season Kmid+i−Lini+dev+midLlate⁢(Kend−Kmid)late season

L denotes stage lengths in days; subscripts with + are cumulative lengths.

The tabulated mid- and end-season values are for a sub-humid climate. Kyzylorda is drier and windier, so they are adjusted every year with the mean wind and minimum humidity of that stage (FAO-56 Eq. 62 and 65). The end-season value is adjusted only when it is at least 0.45.

RHmin

With NASA POWER mid-season wind of about 3.3–3.6 m s⁻¹ and RHmin of 19–23 %, the adjustment raises Kmid by 0.07–0.15.

All coefficients, stage lengths and sowing dates are values from FAO-56 Tables 11 and 12 applied to Kyzylorda Region. They are literature parameters, recorded with status assumed, not local measurements. Where a crop group has no row of its own, a proxy crop describes it.

06

Effective rainfall

Only part of the rain that falls during the season can be used by the crop. Effective rainfall is estimated from monthly totals with the USDA Soil Conservation Service formula.

Effective rainfall, USDA SCSmonthly, mm

Pe= { P⁢(125−0.2⁢P)125P<250 mm/month 125+0.1⁢PP≥250 mm/month

P is the total for the whole calendar month; when the season covers part of a month, Pe is prorated by the number of season days in it.

Rainfall barely matters here. NASA POWER gives about 51 mm for April–October, and effective rainfall is 1.7–5.1 % of seasonal crop demand. The precipitation series cannot be checked against the local station, whose daily rainfall reports are almost entirely missing.

07

Field and canal losses

For every crop except rice, the season is balanced month by month. The net irrigation requirement is the crop demand not covered by effective rainfall. Field losses and canal losses then scale it up to the water applied at the field and the water taken from the river.

Three water levels, per hectareFAO Training Manual 3, §4.3

NIR=∑mmax⁡(ETc,m−Pe,m,0) Wfield=NIRηf Wriver=Wfieldηc

m runs over the months of the season. Each depth in mm becomes a coefficient in m³/ha by the factor 10.

No measured canal or field efficiencies were found for Kyzylorda Region, so both are scenario assumptions. The base values come from FAO guidance for long earth canals and surface irrigation; the ranges span the plausible alternatives. A pessimistic canal efficiency of 0.40 is included as a scenario because a public statement by a senator on regional water losses implies 0.39–0.50. It is a stress test, not a measured value.

08

Rice, flooding and percolation

Rice is grown in flooded basins. Before planting, the soil is saturated and a standing layer of water is established; during the season, water drains through the soil (percolation and seepage). Following FAO Training Manual 3 (§4.4), the water needed at the field is:

Rice: water at the fieldFAO Training Manual 3, §4.4

Wfieldrice=∑mmax⁡ (ETc,m+SATm+WLm +PERC⁢dm−Pe,m,0) , Wriverrice=Wfieldriceηc
SAT
soil saturation before planting: 200 mm, once, in the first month of the season
WL
establishing the standing water layer: 100 mm, once, in the first month
PERC
percolation and seepage: 4 (heavy clay), 6 (base) or 8 (sandy) mm per day
dm
flooded days of the season in month m; the whole 150-day season is treated as flooded

Field application efficiency is not applied to rice. The losses on a rice basin are already represented explicitly by percolation, and applying ηf as well would count them twice. Only canal losses are added between the field and the river. The crop-need level for rice is the same max(ETc − Pe, 0) as for the other crops, so the levels remain comparable.

With these parameters, rice needs 22 200–28 200 m³/ha at the field (percolation 4–8 mm/day), close to the “improved” irrigation norms in the regional literature and below the “existing” norms, which also include drainage releases. Surface releases from rice basins and leaching of saline land are not modelled.

Regional water use

Once the seasonal coefficients exist, regional water use at any level is a weighted sum over the eight crop groups. This is the only calculation the website and the simulator perform.

Cmean is not an average climate. Each of the 16 years 2010–2025 is modelled on its own daily weather, and Cmean is the mean of the 16 resulting coefficients for each crop and scenario. It is a planning reference, not a forecast of a future season.

10

Crop-mix optimiser

The optimiser looks for the crop mix that needs the least river water while staying close to the current structure. It is a linear programme over the eight crop areas, solved with the HiGHS solver through scipy.optimize.linprog. It minimises water, not cost or profit. Income is not modelled, because costs per hectare and several prices and yields are not available.

This section describes the original same-land optimiser, whose results appear on the Simulator and Evidence pages. A separate, more general formulation (water budget, unsown land allowed, other objectives, shadow prices) is described on the Decision Lab page. It does not change the results below.

The solution always sits at a corner of the feasible region: rice moves to the lower edge of its corridor, the other crops to their upper edges, and fodder takes the remaining area. Real farms change their crop mix gradually, so the optimised mixes are bounds on what restructuring alone could do, not recommendations. Rotations, soils, salinity, markets and the fodder needs of livestock are not represented.

11

Baseline versus optimised

The baseline and the optimised mixes are computed differently, and the difference matters for how savings are read.

  • Baseline 2025 is the official 2025 crop structure. It can be evaluated under any climate and efficiency scenario.
  • Optimised mixes are found once, under Cmean and Ebase. Under other conditions the same areas are evaluated, not re-optimised.

Saving under conditions C and Eriver withdrawal

S(C,E)= W(Abase,C,E)−W(Aopt,C,E) W(Abase,C,E) ×100 %

Both mixes are evaluated under the same C and E. Canal efficiency divides every crop’s withdrawal by the same factor, so it cancels out of S: it changes the volumes, not the saving in percent.

Across all 612 conditions (17 climate options × 36 efficiency scenarios), the saving ranges from −0.3 to 3.3 % (Tight), −1.4 to 8.1 % (Moderate) and −2.3 to 13.5 % (Flexible). A negative value means the mix chosen for average conditions uses more water than the baseline under those particular conditions.

12

Water-year classes

Years are classed as low, average or high water by upstream Syr Darya basin runoff, using the 25th and 75th percentiles of the available series. Only five years (2020–2024) of runoff are published in open yearbooks, so the classification is coarse: with five values, the low and high classes each contain exactly one year.

The model does not link a water class to the weather of the same year: a dry year upstream is not assumed to be a hot year downstream.

13

What the method is not

It is

  • A transparent planning model that compares crop mixes under stated climate and efficiency assumptions.
  • A regional model: Kyzylorda Region is one unit.
  • Reproducible: two commands rebuild every result and the website data from the data files.

It is not

  • A forecast of a future season, or a measurement of actual withdrawal.
  • Validated: comparisons with official figures rest on zero or one observation each.
  • A district or field model, an economic model, or a model of deficit irrigation, leaching or drainage releases.
